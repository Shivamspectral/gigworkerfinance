If you Dashed in 2025, the IRS treats you as a self-employed business owner, not an employee. That is annoying in April and useful the rest of the year. Self-employed Dashers can subtract ordinary and necessary business expenses from gig income on Schedule C, which lowers both income tax and the self-employment tax that hits 1099 work.

This guide is a complete, practical list of DoorDash deductions drivers actually use. It is educational, not personalized tax advice. Confirm the current standard mileage rate and any new IRS rules before you file.

## How DoorDash pay shows up at tax time

DoorDash does not withhold federal income tax or Social Security/Medicare. Depending on how much you were paid and how you were paid, you may receive a Form 1099-NEC, a Form 1099-K, both, or (if you were under reporting thresholds) nothing at all. You still have to report the income.

Add up:

- Dasher app deposits
- Customer tips
- Peak pay / promotions
- Any referral bonuses

Then subtract allowable expenses. The difference is your net profit — the number that feeds income tax and self-employment tax.

> Rule of thumb: keep a simple annual spreadsheet with gross DoorDash pay in one column and expenses in the other. Do not wait for the 1099. 1099s are often incomplete or late.

## Vehicle expenses: standard mileage vs. actual costs

For most Dashers, the car is the biggest deduction. You generally choose one method per vehicle:

### 1. Standard mileage rate

For 2025, the IRS business standard mileage rate is **70 cents per mile**. Multiply your documented business miles by that rate. The mileage rate is designed to cover gas, oil, repairs, insurance, and depreciation in one number.

Business miles for DoorDash typically include:

- Miles from your first pickup to your last drop-off while you are on an active dash
- Miles between stacked orders
- Miles driving to a hotspot *if* you can show you were working, not commuting

Miles from your driveway to a random parking lot where you then go online are a gray area. Many tax pros treat the start of your workday as the first business-related stop. Be consistent and keep a log.

### 2. Actual expense method

You add up gas, oil, tires, repairs, insurance, registration, lease payments or depreciation, and then multiply by your **business-use percentage** (business miles ÷ total miles).

Actual expenses can win if you drive an expensive-to-run vehicle relatively few business miles. Mileage is simpler and usually better for high-mileage compact cars. If you have ever used the standard mileage rate on a vehicle you own, switching to actual expenses later can be restricted — read IRS Publication 463 or ask a preparer before you switch.

You can still deduct business parking and tolls under either method.

## The complete DoorDash deduction list for 2025

### Vehicle and delivery gear

- Business miles (standard rate) **or** actual car costs
- Parking fees and tolls while dashing
- Car washes if you keep the car clean for customer-facing work (document the business reason)
- Phone mount, charger, and power inverter
- Insulated pizza bag, drink holder, and hot/cold bags
- Dolly or wagon if you do large grocery-style deliveries
- Winter scraper, flashlight, or safety vest used for night deliveries

The red DoorDash bag is a classic example of an ordinary and necessary expense. Keep the receipt.

### Phone, data, and tech

- Percentage of your cell phone bill used for the Dasher app, navigation, and customer texts
- Percentage of a hotspot or unlimited-data add-on you bought so maps would not die
- App subscriptions you truly use for work (mileage tracker, music-free navigation, scheduling tools)
- A tablet or second cheap phone used only for dashing

Do not deduct 100% of a family phone plan unless that plan exists only for the business. A reasonable business-use percentage (for example 40–70% if you dash many hours) plus a one-line note in your records is more defensible.

### Home office and admin (only if you qualify)

A home-office deduction is allowed only if you use a space **regularly and exclusively** for administrative work — scheduling, bookkeeping, tax prep — and you have no other fixed location for that work. The kitchen table you also eat dinner on does not qualify.

If you do qualify, you can use the simplified method ($5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet) or actual home expenses allocated by square footage.

Other admin costs that do not require a home office:

- Tax software or a paid preparer for the self-employment return
- Bank fees on a dedicated business checking account
- Notebooks, printer ink, and postage for tax records
- A simple LLC filing fee if you formed one for the delivery work (talk to a pro first; an LLC does not by itself change federal self-employment tax)

### Health, retirement, and “above the line” items

These are not Schedule C expenses in the same way mileage is, but they still matter:

- Deductible part of self-employment tax (an automatic adjustment on Form 1040)
- Health insurance premiums if you are self-employed and otherwise eligible
- SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or solo 401(k) contributions

Those last two can shrink taxable income after you know your net profit. They are planning tools, not first-week deductions.

## What you generally cannot deduct

- Speeding tickets, parking tickets, and other fines
- Regular clothes you could wear off the clock
- Commuting miles to a W-2 job
- The full cost of a new car in one year (depreciation rules apply if you use actual expenses)
- Meals you eat alone between orders (the IRS is strict here)
- Personal streaming subscriptions, even if you listen on the road

If an expense would exist in the same amount without DoorDash, it is probably personal.

## Records that make the deduction real

A deduction without a record is a wish. For 2025, keep:

1. **A mileage log** with date, start/end, business miles, and “DoorDash” as the purpose. A GPS app is fine if you can export the data.
2. **Receipts** for gear, car work, and phone bills. Photograph paper receipts the same day.
3. **A year-end profit snapshot**: platform totals + cash tips + PayPal/Venmo oddballs.
4. **Total miles on the odometer** on January 1 and December 31 (needed for business-use percentage).

The IRS can ask for contemporaneous logs. That means you recorded miles as you drove, not reconstructed them from memory in March.

## A simple year-end checklist

- Download your Dasher earnings reports for every month
- Export mileage from your tracker and back it up as a CSV or PDF
- Add up hot bags, mounts, and other gear
- Calculate phone business-use percentage
- Decide mileage vs. actual expenses
- Set aside money for the tax bill (see our savings guide)
- Book a preparer in February if your return is more than a simple Schedule C

## Bottom line

DoorDash deductions are not exotic. They are mileage, gear, a slice of your phone, parking, tolls, and clean records. The drivers who overpay are usually the ones who either deduct nothing or deduct everything. Stay in the middle: ordinary, necessary, documented.

If you also drive Uber, Lyft, Instacart, or Grubhub, you can use the same list. Combine the income and expenses on one Schedule C if it is all the same delivery/rideshare activity, or ask a preparer how to present mixed gig work cleanly.
