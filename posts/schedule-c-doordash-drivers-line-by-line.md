## What Schedule C is and why every Dasher files one

Schedule C (Profit or Loss From Business) is the form where you report your DoorDash income, subtract your business expenses, and arrive at the net profit that actually gets taxed. Every Dasher who earned money during the year files one – whether or not DoorDash issued a 1099.

Tax software fills this in through an interview, but knowing what each line is for makes it much easier to spot when the software has put something in the wrong place. This walkthrough follows the form top to bottom. For the overall process around it, see [how to file DoorDash taxes](/blog/how-to-file-doordash-taxes).

## The header section

**Line A – Principal business or profession.** Something plain like "Food delivery driver" or "Delivery services" is fine.

**Line B – Business code.** Use **492000** (Couriers and Messengers). Some software offers 492210 (Local Messengers and Local Delivery); either is acceptable and neither affects your tax.

**Line C – Business name.** Leave blank unless you've set up a business entity. Your own name is the default.

**Line D – EIN.** Leave blank if you don't have one. Your SSN is enough.

**Line E – Business address.** Your home address.

**Line F – Accounting method.** Cash. You report income when you receive it and expenses when you pay them.

**Line G – Material participation.** Yes. You personally did the work.

**Line H.** Check this only if this is your first year dashing.

**Line I – Did you make payments requiring a 1099?** No, for the vast majority of Dashers.

## Part I – Income

**Line 1 – Gross receipts or sales.** Your total DoorDash earnings for the year: base pay, promotions, and app tips, plus any cash tips. If you have a 1099-NEC, this number should be at least the amount in Box 1. If you didn't get one, use your Dasher app earnings total – [how to get your DoorDash 1099](/blog/how-to-get-doordash-1099) explains both cases.

If you also drove for Uber Eats, Grubhub, or Instacart, all of it goes on this same line. Delivery across multiple platforms is one business, not several.

**Lines 2–6.** Returns, cost of goods sold, and other income don't apply to delivery work. Leave them at zero.

**Line 7 – Gross income.** Same as Line 1 for a Dasher.

## Part II – Expenses

This is where your deductions go. The [DoorDash deductions list](/blog/doordash-tax-deductions-2025) covers what qualifies; this section covers which line each one lands on.

**Line 9 – Car and truck expenses.** The big one. If you use the standard mileage rate, multiply your business miles by the IRS rate for the year (70 cents per mile for 2025) and enter the result here. If you use the actual-expense method, your fuel, insurance, repairs, and depreciation total goes here instead. You can't do both. Tolls and parking during deliveries also go on this line under either method.

**Line 18 – Office expense.** Rarely used by Dashers. Skip unless you have genuine office costs.

**Line 22 – Supplies.** Insulated bags, drink carriers, phone mounts, chargers, and similar gear.

**Line 24a – Travel.** Overnight travel only. Local driving does not go here.

**Line 24b – Meals.** Food you buy for yourself while dashing is **not** deductible. Leave this at zero unless you have a genuine business meal with a client, which almost never applies to delivery.

**Line 25 – Utilities.** The business-use percentage of your phone plan goes here (some preparers put it on Line 27a instead – either is fine, just don't do both).

**Line 27a – Other expenses.** Anything that doesn't fit above: mileage-tracking app subscriptions, a portion of roadside assistance, background check fees, business-use portion of a new phone. You'll list each item in Part V, and the total carries to this line.

**Line 28 – Total expenses.** Sum of Lines 8 through 27a.

## Part II – The bottom line

**Line 29 – Tentative profit.** Line 7 minus Line 28.

**Line 30 – Home office.** Almost no Dasher qualifies. A space used only for tracking receipts a few minutes a week doesn't meet the regular-and-exclusive-use test. Skip it.

**Line 31 – Net profit or loss.** This is the number everything else is built on. It flows to:

- **Schedule 1, Line 3**, which feeds into your Form 1040 income
- **Schedule SE**, which calculates your self-employment tax

If Line 31 is $400 or more, you owe self-employment tax. The [self-employment tax explainer](/blog/self-employment-tax-explained-gig-drivers) shows how that's calculated and why half of it comes back as a deduction on Schedule 1.

**Line 32.** Only relevant if you show a loss. Most Dashers check 32a (all investment is at risk).

## Part IV – Information on your vehicle

If you claimed car expenses on Line 9 and are not filing Form 4562, you must complete this section. Software will ask for it; if you're on paper, don't skip it – a Line 9 deduction with a blank Part IV is a common reason for correspondence from the IRS.

**Line 43.** The date you started using the car for dashing.

**Line 44.** Total miles for the year split into business, commuting, and other. Your business figure should match what you used on Line 9. This is why a mileage log matters – see [best free mileage tracker apps](/blog/best-free-mileage-tracker-apps) if you don't have one running yet.

**Lines 45–47.** Whether the car was available for personal use (yes), whether you have another vehicle (answer honestly), and whether you have written evidence for your mileage (you should be able to say yes).

## Part V – Other expenses

List each item that rolled up into Line 27a, with a short description and amount. Keep the descriptions plain: "Mileage tracking app subscription – $60", "Phone (business-use portion) – $180".

## The five most common Schedule C mistakes Dashers make

1. **Reporting net instead of gross on Line 1.** Line 1 is before expenses. The deductions come later.
2. **Claiming standard mileage and actual car expenses.** Pick one method per vehicle per year.
3. **Deducting personal meals on Line 24b.** Not allowed.
4. **Leaving Part IV blank.** Required whenever you claim vehicle expenses without Form 4562.
5. **Filing one Schedule C per app.** All delivery and rideshare work goes on a single Schedule C.

More of these, and how to avoid them, are in [the biggest tax mistakes gig drivers make](/blog/biggest-tax-mistakes-gig-drivers).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need Form 4562?**
Only if you're using the actual-expense method and claiming depreciation, or if it's the first year you're claiming the car and your software requires it. Standard-mileage filers can usually complete Part IV instead.

**What if my Schedule C shows a loss?**
A genuine loss offsets other income on your 1040. But a delivery business that shows losses year after year can be reclassified as a hobby, which eliminates the deductions. Make sure your mileage is accurate rather than inflated.

**Can I file Schedule C for free?**
Several free options support it – see [free tax filing options for gig workers](/blog/free-tax-filing-options-gig-workers). Watch out for tools that advertise "free" but charge the moment you add self-employment income.

**How much should I have set aside to cover Line 31?**
Depends on your bracket, but the [how much to save for taxes](/blog/how-much-to-save-for-taxes-gig-driver) guide gives a method for working it out from your net profit rather than a flat guess.
