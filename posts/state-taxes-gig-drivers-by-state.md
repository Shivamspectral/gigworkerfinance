## Federal taxes are the same everywhere - state taxes are not

Every gig driver in the US pays the same federal self-employment tax and federal income tax rates regardless of where they live. State income taxes are an entirely separate layer that varies dramatically - from zero in some states to over 13% at higher income levels in others.

Understanding your state's tax situation affects how much you should save, whether you need to make state estimated payments, and what to do if you drive in multiple states.

## States with no income tax (biggest advantage for gig drivers)

Nine states currently have no state income tax on wages or self-employment income:

- Alaska
- Florida
- Nevada
- New Hampshire (taxes interest and dividends but not earned income)
- South Dakota
- Tennessee (phased out income tax fully as of 2022)
- Texas
- Washington
- Wyoming

If you live and work in one of these states, your tax savings rate can be slightly lower than the standard 25-30% recommendation since you only owe federal taxes. Many drivers in these states find 22-25% adequate.

## High income tax states: save more

Several states impose significant income tax that gig drivers must account for:

| State | Top marginal rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| California | 13.3% | Highest in the nation, applies to higher incomes |
| Hawaii | 11% | High rate, limited deductions |
| New Jersey | 10.75% | High top rate |
| Oregon | 9.9% | Applies at relatively moderate income levels |
| Minnesota | 9.85% | Strong middle-class tax burden |
| New York | 10.9% (plus NYC surcharge) | NYC adds additional local tax |

Drivers in these states should save 30-35% of gross income to cover both federal and state obligations comfortably.

## State estimated tax payments

Most states with income tax mirror the federal quarterly payment system, requiring estimated payments if you expect to owe a certain threshold (often $500 to $1,000 depending on the state, compared to the federal $1,000 threshold).

**Important:** State estimated payments go to your state tax agency separately from federal payments to the IRS. They are not the same payment. Many new gig drivers correctly make federal quarterly payments and forget state entirely until filing.

To find your state's estimated payment portal and deadlines, search your state's name plus "estimated tax payments" - most states have an online payment system similar to IRS Direct Pay.

## Multi-state driving: do you owe taxes in multiple states

Generally, you owe income tax to the state where you are a resident (where you live), not necessarily every state you drive in. However, some specific situations create multi-state complexity:

**Living near a state border and regularly driving in a neighboring state:**
Some states have reciprocity agreements with neighboring states, meaning you only file in your home state. Others do not, potentially creating a filing obligation in both states.

**Moving states mid-year:**
You may owe partial-year returns in both your old and new state, prorated based on when you lived in each.

**Driving for extended periods in another state:**
If you spend significant time driving in a state other than your home state (common for drivers who spend months in a warmer state, for example), that state may consider you to have established nexus and a filing obligation there.

For most gig drivers who live and work in a single state year-round, this complexity does not apply. If your situation involves regular cross-border activity, a tax professional familiar with multi-state returns is worth consulting.

## State-specific deductions and credits for self-employed workers

Some states offer deductions or credits that specifically benefit self-employed workers beyond the federal deductions:

- Several states allow deduction of self-employment tax paid (similar to the federal deduction for half of SE tax)
- Some states have small business tax credits that sole proprietors may qualify for
- State retirement contribution deductions sometimes differ from federal treatment

Check your state's department of revenue website or use state-specific tax software guidance to identify any state-level benefits available to you.

## California specifically: extra considerations for gig drivers

California deserves special mention because of its combination of high tax rates and specific rules:

- California's AB5 law (and subsequent Prop 22 for app-based drivers specifically) created ongoing legal complexity around driver classification - currently rideshare and delivery drivers remain classified as independent contractors under Prop 22
- California has its own Self-Employment Disability Insurance (SDI) program that self-employed workers can optionally participate in
- California requires estimated payments if you expect to owe $500 or more in state tax
- The California Franchise Tax Board (FTB) has strong enforcement and matching systems - ensure all 1099 income is reported accurately

## New York City specifically: the local tax layer

If you drive in New York City and are a resident, you owe city income tax in addition to state income tax - a layer of taxation most other US cities do not impose on this scale. NYC resident gig drivers should factor in an additional 3-3.876% local tax rate on top of state and federal obligations when calculating their savings rate.

## How to handle state taxes practically

**Step 1:** Confirm whether your state has income tax and look up the rate that applies to your expected income level.

**Step 2:** Adjust your savings rate accordingly:
- No income tax state: 22-25% total savings rate
- Low-moderate income tax state: 25-28%
- High income tax state (California, New York, etc.): 30-35%

**Step 3:** Set up state estimated payments if required, on your state's online payment portal, using the same quarterly schedule (or your state's equivalent schedule) as federal payments.

**Step 4:** When filing your annual return, use tax software that handles both federal and state returns together to ensure deductions and credits are properly applied at both levels.

## Frequently asked questions

**If I live in a no-income-tax state but make a delivery to a neighboring state, do I owe that state's taxes?**
For occasional cross-border deliveries, generally no - your tax home is your state of residence. Consistent regular work in another state is a different analysis.

**Does my state tax apply to tips I receive through the app?**
Yes, tips are taxable income at both federal and state levels.

**Can I deduct state income taxes paid on my federal return?**
You can deduct state income taxes as an itemized deduction on Schedule A, but only if you itemize rather than take the standard deduction, and the SALT (state and local tax) deduction is currently capped at $10,000 for most filers.

**What if I cannot afford to pay my state tax bill?**
Most states offer payment plans similar to the IRS. File your return on time regardless, then contact your state tax agency to arrange a payment agreement. Failure to file penalties are typically steeper than failure to pay penalties.

## Bottom line

State income taxes add a meaningful layer to gig driver tax obligations that varies enormously by location. Drivers in no-income-tax states can save slightly less, while those in California, New York, and similar high-tax states need to save 30-35% of gross income to avoid a significant underpayment. Make state estimated payments separately from federal payments, and factor your state's specific rate into every savings and quarterly payment calculation.
