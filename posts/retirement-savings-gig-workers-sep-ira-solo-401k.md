## Gig drivers have access to powerful retirement accounts most people do not know about

W-2 employees typically access retirement savings through employer-sponsored 401(k) plans. As a self-employed gig driver, you do not have an employer offering this, but you have access to retirement account options that are arguably more flexible and potentially allow higher contributions than a typical workplace plan.

The two main options for self-employed workers are the SEP-IRA and the Solo 401(k). Both reduce your taxable income dollar for dollar and let investment grow tax-deferred - the same fundamental benefit as any retirement account, just structured for independent workers.

## Why retirement contributions matter for tax purposes right now

Beyond the long-term benefit of saving for retirement, contributions to these accounts reduce your adjusted gross income in the current tax year. For a gig driver in the 22% federal tax bracket, a $5,000 contribution saves approximately $1,100 in federal income tax in the year the contribution is made, plus reduces the income subject to state income tax where applicable.

This is money you would have paid in taxes that instead goes into your own retirement account. The tax savings make the effective cost of contributing significantly lower than the contribution amount itself.

## SEP-IRA: the simpler option

SEP stands for Simplified Employee Pension. Despite the name, it works perfectly well for solo self-employed workers with no employees.

**Contribution limits for 2025:**
You can contribute up to 25% of your net self-employment income (after the self-employment tax deduction), with a maximum contribution of $69,000 for 2025.

**Example contributions at different income levels:**

| Net self-employment income | Maximum SEP-IRA contribution |
|---|---|
| $20,000 | $3,706 |
| $35,000 | $6,588 |
| $50,000 | $9,293 |
| $80,000 | $14,842 |

**How to open one:**
SEP-IRAs are available at virtually every major brokerage - Fidelity, Vanguard, Charles Schwab, and others offer them with no account fees. The process involves completing a simple form (IRS Form 5305-SEP or the brokerage's equivalent) and opening the account. This takes about 20 minutes online.

**Contribution deadline:**
You can make SEP-IRA contributions for a tax year up until your tax filing deadline including extensions, giving you until October of the following year if you file an extension. This flexibility means you can wait until you know your actual annual income before deciding the contribution amount.

**Investment options:**
Once funded, you invest the money in whatever investments the brokerage offers - index funds, ETFs, stocks, bonds. Most drivers starting out do well with a simple low-cost index fund.

## Solo 401(k): higher contributions at lower income levels

The Solo 401(k) (also called Individual 401(k) or Self-Employed 401(k)) is structured differently from a SEP-IRA and allows higher contributions at moderate income levels.

**How contributions work:**
A Solo 401(k) has two contribution components:

1. **Employee contribution:** Up to $23,500 for 2025 (or $31,000 if age 50 or older), regardless of income level, as long as you have at least that much in net self-employment income
2. **Employer contribution:** Up to 25% of net self-employment income on top of the employee contribution

**Why this matters at moderate income:**

| Net income | SEP-IRA max | Solo 401(k) max |
|---|---|---|
| $30,000 | $5,576 | $23,500 (employee only) |
| $50,000 | $9,293 | $23,500 + $9,293 = $32,793 |
| $80,000 | $14,842 | $23,500 + $14,842 = $38,342 |

At $30,000 net income, a Solo 401(k) allows nearly four times the contribution of a SEP-IRA - a significant difference if maximizing tax deductions is a priority.

**Complexity tradeoff:**
Solo 401(k)s involve slightly more administrative setup and paperwork, and once the account balance exceeds $250,000, annual IRS reporting (Form 5500-EZ) is required. For most gig drivers starting out, this is a future consideration rather than an immediate concern.

**Contribution deadline:**
Unlike the SEP-IRA, the employee contribution portion of a Solo 401(k) must be made by December 31 of the tax year. The employer contribution portion can be made up to the tax filing deadline including extensions.

**Where to open one:**
Fidelity offers a no-fee Solo 401(k). Vanguard and Schwab also offer them, though Fidelity's version is often recommended for its combination of no fees and broad investment options.

## Which should you choose

**Choose SEP-IRA if:**
- You want the simplest possible setup and administration
- Your income is higher (above $80,000 net self-employment income) where SEP-IRA contributions approach Solo 401(k) levels anyway
- You are not sure how much you want to contribute and want flexibility to decide after year-end

**Choose Solo 401(k) if:**
- Your net self-employment income is under $80,000 and you want to maximize contributions and tax savings
- You are 50 or older and want to take advantage of catch-up contribution provisions
- You want the option to take loans from your retirement account (Solo 401(k) allows this, SEP-IRA does not)

## Starting small is still worth it

You do not need to maximize contributions to benefit. Even a $1,000 or $2,000 annual contribution into either account:
- Reduces your tax bill by $220 to $440 at the 22% bracket
- Starts building investment compounding that grows over years
- Establishes the habit of treating retirement savings as a real financial priority

The perfect contribution amount is whatever you can make consistently.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I have both a SEP-IRA and a Solo 401(k)?**
Generally not for the same self-employment income, as contribution limits apply across both accounts combined. You typically choose one structure for your primary self-employment retirement savings.

**What if I have employees - can I still use these accounts?**
Solo 401(k) is restricted to businesses with no employees other than the owner (and spouse). SEP-IRA requires you to make proportional contributions for eligible employees if you have them. Most gig drivers operate alone, making this a non-issue.

**Do contributions affect my self-employment tax?**
SEP-IRA and Solo 401(k) contributions reduce income tax but generally do not reduce self-employment tax, which is calculated at the net profit level before retirement deductions.

**When can I access the money without penalty?**
Both accounts follow standard retirement account rules - withdrawals before age 59½ typically incur a 10% early withdrawal penalty plus ordinary income tax on the amount withdrawn, with certain exceptions.

## Bottom line

Both SEP-IRA and Solo 401(k) offer gig drivers genuine tax savings today alongside retirement security for the future. The SEP-IRA wins on simplicity while the Solo 401(k) wins on contribution limits at moderate income levels. Either option is dramatically better than no retirement savings at all, and the tax reduction in the contribution year makes them one of the most financially efficient moves a self-employed driver can make.
