## What changes when you earn from more than one app

Multi-apping is good for your hourly rate. It's harder on your tax return – not because the rules are different, but because your income and mileage are now split across platforms that don't talk to each other.

If you're new to running two apps at once, start with the [multi-apping guide](/blog/multi-apping-uber-doordash-guide). This post covers the record-keeping side so April doesn't hurt.

## You'll get a 1099 from each platform – but the IRS sees one business

Each platform that pays you above its reporting threshold sends its own form – a 1099-NEC or 1099-K depending on how the platform reports. That's normal. The mistake is treating each app as a separate business.

For tax purposes, delivery and rideshare driving across Uber, DoorDash, Grubhub, and Instacart is one self-employment activity. You report all of it on a single Schedule C, and self-employment tax is calculated on the **combined** net profit.

**Why this matters:** two "modest" app incomes combined can push you well past the point where quarterly estimated payments are required, even if neither app alone would have. If you're only glancing at one app's earnings summary, you'll underestimate what you owe. The [self-employment tax explainer](/blog/self-employment-tax-explained-gig-drivers) walks through how that combined number gets taxed.

## In-app mileage trackers miss the miles between apps

DoorDash and Uber each log miles only while you're actively on a delivery in *their* app. When you're multi-apping, a lot of your driving falls outside both:

- Driving from a DoorDash drop-off to an Uber Eats pickup
- Sitting or cruising with both apps open waiting for a ping
- Driving back to your zone at the end of a session

Those are legitimate business miles, and at the IRS standard mileage rate they add up to a meaningful deduction. But only if something is actually recording them.

**Fix:** Run one independent mileage tracker continuously for your whole shift, regardless of which app is active. Our roundup of [free mileage tracker apps](/blog/best-free-mileage-tracker-apps) covers the options, and the [Stride walkthrough](/blog/how-to-use-stride-app-taxes) shows the setup if you want the simplest free choice.

## Don't add up the in-app numbers and call it your total

A common shortcut: take DoorDash's reported mileage, add Uber's reported mileage, and use that sum as your business miles. This almost always **undercounts**, because it misses every connector mile described above.

Use your independent tracker as the source of truth. Treat the in-app figures as a rough cross-check only. If your tracker shows noticeably more than the two app totals combined, that's expected – it means it caught the gaps.

## Track income per platform, but total it monthly

Keep a simple spreadsheet (or an expense app – see [best expense tracking apps for gig workers](/blog/best-expense-tracking-apps-gig-workers)) with one column per platform and a combined total row at the end of each month.

The per-platform columns let you reconcile against each 1099 in January. The combined row is what you actually use for quarterly estimates. Our [quarterly estimated taxes guide](/blog/quarterly-estimated-taxes-gig-workers) covers the deadlines and how to calculate the payment – just make sure the income figure you plug in is the combined one.

## Deduct shared expenses once, based on total business use

Insulated bags, phone mount, phone plan, car washes, maintenance, and (if you use the actual-expense method instead of standard mileage) fuel and depreciation are shared across every app you drive for.

Deduct each of them **once** on Schedule C, with the business-use percentage based on your total gig driving – not split per platform, and definitely not claimed separately for each app. The [DoorDash deductions list](/blog/doordash-tax-deductions-2025) and [Uber deductions list](/blog/uber-driver-tax-deductions-2025) overlap heavily for exactly this reason; you're claiming one set of expenses, not two.

## Raise your tax set-aside when you add a second app

A flat "save 25–30%" rule that worked when you drove one app often falls short once you're multi-apping, because the combined income can land you in a higher bracket. Recalculate using your projected **combined** annual net profit. [How much to save for taxes as a gig driver](/blog/how-much-to-save-for-taxes-gig-driver) walks through the math.

## Reconcile your 1099s against your own records in January

When the forms arrive, compare each platform's reported gross to your own per-platform column. Small differences are usually timing (a late-December payout landing in January). Large differences need checking before you file – see [how to handle a wrong 1099-K](/blog/how-to-handle-wrong-1099-k) if a platform's number doesn't match reality.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a separate Schedule C for each app?**
No. Delivery and rideshare across multiple platforms is the same type of work and goes on one Schedule C. You'd only add a second Schedule C for a genuinely different business (for example, freelance design alongside driving).

**Will multiple 1099s trigger an audit?**
Multiple 1099s from gig platforms are extremely common and not an audit flag on their own. What matters is that your reported gross income is at least the sum of all your 1099s.

**Can I deduct miles driven with both apps open but no active order?**
Yes, if you're genuinely available for work and positioned to take orders – that's business use. Commuting from home to your zone before you turn any app on is the grey area; many drivers turn an app on before leaving to make the trip clearly business-related. Keep a log either way.

**What's the single biggest multi-apping tax mistake?**
Underpaying quarterly estimates because you were mentally tracking one app's income at a time, then getting hit with the [underpayment penalty](/blog/quarterly-tax-penalty-gig-workers) in April.
