## Why this matters more than picking the right app

Every deduction you claim on Schedule C is a claim the IRS can ask you to prove. If you can't, the deduction is disallowed, you owe the tax plus interest, and possibly a penalty. For gig drivers the biggest deduction is almost always mileage, which means the single most important record you own is a mileage log – not a shoebox of gas receipts.

An expense tracking app makes all of this easier, and [best expense tracking apps for gig workers](/blog/best-expense-tracking-apps-gig-workers) compares the options. But the app only helps if you know what it needs to capture. This post covers that.

## The one record you cannot skip: a contemporaneous mileage log

"Contemporaneous" means recorded at or near the time of the trip – not reconstructed in April from memory. The IRS has disallowed mileage deductions specifically because the log was written after the fact, even when the miles were probably real.

For each business trip your log needs:

- **Date**
- **Starting and ending odometer reading, or total miles**
- **Where you went** (a general area is fine for delivery – "downtown deliveries" or "zone around Main St")
- **Business purpose** ("DoorDash deliveries", "Uber rides")

You also need your **total miles for the year** (all driving, personal included) because Schedule C Part IV asks for business, commuting, and other miles separately. Write down your odometer on January 1 and December 31, or take a photo of it.

A GPS tracking app does all of this automatically, which is why it's the first thing worth setting up – [best free mileage tracker apps](/blog/best-free-mileage-tracker-apps) covers the options and the [Stride walkthrough](/blog/how-to-use-stride-app-taxes) shows the setup. If you'd rather log manually, a notebook in the glovebox or a phone note filled in at the end of each session is acceptable, as long as you actually do it that day.

## Standard mileage vs actual expenses: different records required

Which method you use changes what you need to keep.

**Standard mileage rate.** You multiply business miles by the IRS rate. You do **not** need gas, insurance, or repair receipts to claim it – those costs are baked into the rate. You still need to keep receipts for tolls and parking, which are deductible on top.

**Actual expense method.** You deduct the business-use percentage of every vehicle cost: fuel, insurance, repairs, tyres, registration, lease payments or depreciation. Now you need receipts for **all** of it, plus the same mileage log to establish what percentage of your driving was business.

Most delivery and rideshare drivers use standard mileage because it's simpler and usually larger. If you're deciding between the two, [lease or buy a car for gig driving](/blog/lease-or-buy-car-gig-driving) touches on when actual expenses can win.

## What counts as a receipt

For non-vehicle expenses – insulated bags, phone mounts, chargers, app subscriptions, the business portion of your phone plan – the IRS wants documentation showing:

- What you bought
- How much it cost
- When you bought it
- That it was for business

An itemised receipt covers all four. A bank or credit-card statement shows the amount and date but not *what* you bought, so on its own it's weaker evidence. In practice a statement line reading "AMAZON $34.99" plus the order confirmation email is fine. A statement line with nothing else is a gamble.

**Digital copies are fully acceptable.** A phone photo of a paper receipt is as good as the original. You do not need to keep paper.

**The $75 rule.** The IRS doesn't require a receipt for most business expenses under $75 – you can rely on a note in your records with the amount, date, and purpose. Two caveats: lodging always needs a receipt, and many tax professionals advise keeping receipts anyway because a pattern of undocumented small expenses looks worse in an audit than a folder of photos.

## Records you should also keep

- **Earnings summaries** from each platform for the full year (download the weekly or monthly reports, don't rely on the app staying accessible)
- **Your 1099s** – see [how to get your DoorDash 1099](/blog/how-to-get-doordash-1099) if you're missing one
- **Cash tip log** – cash tips are taxable and won't appear anywhere else
- **Proof of quarterly estimated payments** – IRS confirmation numbers or bank records; you'll need them to reconcile in April. The [quarterly estimated taxes guide](/blog/quarterly-estimated-taxes-gig-workers) covers where to find these
- **A copy of the filed return** and every schedule attached to it

## How long to keep everything

The IRS baseline is **three years** from the date you filed the return (or the due date, whichever is later). That's how long they normally have to audit.

It stretches to **six years** if you under-reported gross income by more than 25%, and there is **no limit** if a return is fraudulent or never filed. Because you can't know in advance which window applies, most preparers recommend a simple rule: **keep everything for seven years.**

Two exceptions run longer:

- **Vehicle records** if you're depreciating a car under the actual expense method – keep purchase documents and depreciation schedules for as long as you own the car, plus three years after you sell or stop using it
- **Records supporting a loss carryforward** – keep until three years after the year the loss is fully used

## A five-minute weekly system

You don't need a complicated setup. This works:

1. **Mileage app runs automatically.** Open it once a week, classify any unclassified trips, done.
2. **Photograph receipts the day you get them.** Either into your expense app or into a single cloud folder named by year – "Gig Taxes 2025".
3. **Sunday reconciliation.** Five minutes: log any cash tips from the week, screenshot each platform's weekly earnings summary into the same folder, check your mileage total looks sane.
4. **Monthly total.** Add the week's income and expenses to a running spreadsheet or let your app do it. This is the number you'll use for [working out how much to set aside for taxes](/blog/how-much-to-save-for-taxes-gig-driver).
5. **January export.** Download the full year from your app, save a PDF, and drop it in the folder alongside your 1099s.

Doing this every week means tax season is an hour of assembly rather than a weekend of archaeology. It's also the difference between an audit that ends with a letter and one that ends with a bill.

## What an audit actually looks like for a gig driver

Most gig-driver audits are correspondence audits – a letter asking you to substantiate specific items, usually vehicle expenses. You send copies of your mileage log, your odometer readings, and any receipts they've asked about. If the log is contemporaneous and the numbers tie to your return, that's normally the end of it.

Where drivers lose is predictable: a log that was clearly written in one sitting, business miles that exceed total miles driven, or a Schedule C Part IV left blank. The [biggest tax mistakes gig drivers make](/blog/biggest-tax-mistakes-gig-drivers) covers the rest of that list.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the mileage DoorDash or Uber reports in the app as my log?**
It's better than nothing, but it only captures miles while you're on an active order in that app, so it undercounts. It also isn't a log in the IRS sense – it doesn't show dates and purposes per trip in a form you control. Run your own tracker and use the app figures as a cross-check.

**Do I need receipts if I take the standard mileage deduction?**
Not for fuel, insurance, or maintenance. You do need the mileage log, plus receipts for tolls, parking, and any non-vehicle expenses you claim.

**Is a credit card statement enough proof?**
It proves amount and date. It doesn't prove what you bought or that it was for business. Pair it with the receipt or order confirmation where you can.

**What if I already lost this year's receipts?**
Reconstruct what you can from bank statements, order histories, and email confirmations, and start photographing receipts today. For mileage, if you have no log at all, be conservative – claiming miles you can't support is the fastest way to turn a small deduction into a large problem.

**Should I keep records in a business bank account?**
Running gig income and expenses through a separate account isn't required, but it makes every question above easier to answer. [Business bank accounts for gig drivers](/blog/business-bank-account-gig-drivers) covers the free options.
