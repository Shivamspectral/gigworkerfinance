## What self-employment tax actually is

When you work a regular job, your employer splits Social Security and Medicare taxes with you. You pay 7.65% from your paycheck, and your employer pays another 7.65% on your behalf. As a gig driver, you are both the employee and the employer, which means you pay both halves - the full 15.3%.

This is self-employment tax. It is not a penalty for working independently. It is the same Social Security and Medicare contribution every working American makes, just structured differently for people without a traditional employer.

## The calculation step by step

The IRS does not apply self-employment tax to your full gross income. The calculation involves a small adjustment first.

**Step 1:** Calculate your net profit
Net profit = gross gig income minus all deductible business expenses (mileage, supplies, phone, etc.)

**Step 2:** Multiply net profit by 92.35%
This adjustment exists because employees only pay their 7.65% share on their wages, not on their employer's matching contribution. The 92.35% factor approximates this same principle for self-employed people.

**Step 3:** Apply the 15.3% rate to that adjusted figure
- 12.4% goes toward Social Security (on the first $176,100 of net earnings in 2025)
- 2.9% goes toward Medicare (no income cap)
- An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies above $200,000 for single filers (uncommon for most gig drivers)

**Example calculation:**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross gig income | $45,000 |
| Business deductions (mileage, expenses) | $14,000 |
| Net profit | $31,000 |
| Net profit x 92.35% | $28,629 |
| Self-employment tax (15.3%) | $4,380 |

## The deduction that partially offsets it

The IRS allows you to deduct half of your self-employment tax as an adjustment to income on Form 1040. In the example above, that means deducting $2,190 from your gross income before calculating regular income tax.

This does not reduce the self-employment tax itself, but it slightly reduces the income tax you owe on top of it. Tax software calculates this automatically.

## Why deductions matter so much for self-employment tax

Every dollar of deductible business expense reduces your net profit, which is the base that self-employment tax applies to. This is why mileage tracking is so financially important.

**Example of mileage impact on self-employment tax:**

| Scenario | Net profit | SE tax (approx) |
|---|---|---|
| No mileage tracked | $45,000 | $6,358 |
| 15,000 miles tracked ($10,500 deduction) | $34,500 | $4,878 |
| 20,000 miles tracked ($14,000 deduction) | $31,000 | $4,380 |

The difference between no tracking and full tracking in this example is nearly $2,000 in self-employment tax alone, before considering regular income tax savings on top.

## How self-employment tax interacts with income tax

Self-employment tax and income tax are separate calculations that both apply to your net profit. They stack on top of each other.

For a single filer with $31,000 in net self-employment income and no other income:

| Tax | Amount |
|---|---|
| Self-employment tax | $4,380 |
| Federal income tax (after standard deduction and SE deduction) | approximately $1,800 |
| Total federal tax burden | approximately $6,180 |

This combined burden is why the 25-30% savings rate recommendation exists - it accounts for both taxes together, not just one or the other.

## Does self-employment tax contribute to Social Security benefits

Yes. Self-employment tax payments count toward your Social Security earnings record in exactly the same way W-2 withholding does. Higher lifetime earnings (and therefore higher contributions) generally translate to higher Social Security benefits at retirement, though the formula is complex and other factors apply.

This means self-employment tax is not purely a cost with no return - it is building Social Security credit that eventually benefits you, the same as any other worker.

## Strategies to legally reduce self-employment tax

**1. Maximize deductions**
Every deductible expense reduces net profit and therefore self-employment tax. Mileage, supplies, phone, health insurance premiums, and retirement contributions all play a role.

**2. Contribute to a SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k)**
Retirement contributions reduce your adjusted gross income and the income subject to regular income tax, though they do not directly reduce self-employment tax (which is calculated before retirement deductions apply in most cases).

**3. Consider S-Corp election (advanced, higher income only)**
At higher income levels (generally above $60,000-80,000 in net self-employment income), forming an S-Corporation and paying yourself a reasonable salary can reduce the portion of income subject to self-employment tax. This involves significant additional complexity, cost, and administrative burden - worth evaluating with a CPA only if your income justifies it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is self-employment tax the same as income tax?**
No, they are separate. Self-employment tax covers Social Security and Medicare. Income tax is calculated separately based on your taxable income after deductions. Both apply simultaneously.

**Do I owe self-employment tax if I only made $500 from gig work?**
Self-employment tax applies once net self-employment income reaches $400. Below that threshold, you are not required to file Schedule SE or pay self-employment tax on that income.

**Why does my total tax bill feel so much higher than when I had a regular job?**
When employed, your employer paid half of Social Security and Medicare invisibly - you never saw that cost. As a self-employed person, both halves show up on your tax return explicitly, making the total feel higher even though the underlying contribution rate is the same.

**Can I avoid self-employment tax by forming an LLC?**
A single-member LLC is treated as a sole proprietorship by default for tax purposes, meaning self-employment tax still applies the same way. Only an S-Corp election changes this calculation, and only under specific circumstances worth evaluating with a tax professional.

## Bottom line

Self-employment tax is 15.3% of 92.35% of your net gig income - covering Social Security and Medicare that an employer would otherwise split with you. Maximizing deductions, particularly mileage, directly reduces the net profit this tax applies to, making thorough expense tracking one of the most financially impactful habits a gig driver can build.
