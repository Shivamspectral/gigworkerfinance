## Uber does not withhold taxes - here is what you can deduct instead

Uber pays drivers as independent contractors. No federal tax, no state tax, no Social Security or Medicare comes out of your weekly deposit. In exchange, the tax code lets you deduct real business expenses that reduce the income you actually owe tax on.

Most active Uber drivers can claim between $8,000 and $18,000 in deductions per year, depending on mileage and vehicle costs. This guide covers every deduction category specific to rideshare driving.

## Mileage: the deduction that matters most

The IRS standard mileage rate for 2025 is 70 cents per mile. For Uber drivers this is almost always the biggest deduction on the return.

**Miles that count:**
- From the moment you go online and are available for a ride request
- Driving to pick up a passenger after accepting a request
- The trip itself, passenger in the car
- Miles driven between drop-off and your next ride while still online

**Miles that do not count:**
- Driving from home to your first online session of the day
- Personal trips, errands, or driving home after going offline
- Any miles logged while the Uber app is closed

**Example:** An Uber driver logging 22,000 business miles in 2025 claims 22,000 x $0.70 = $15,400 in mileage deductions alone.

## Phone and data plan

Your phone runs the Uber app, GPS, and rider communication. That makes a portion of your phone bill a legitimate business expense.

Calculate the business-use percentage by comparing hours used for Uber versus total phone use. A driver working 25 hours a week on Uber out of roughly 60 waking hours of phone use might reasonably claim 35 to 40 percent of the monthly bill.

**Also deductible:**
- Phone mount/holder
- Car charger and charging cables
- A backup battery pack used during shifts

## Vehicle maintenance and supplies

If you use the actual expense method instead of standard mileage (you must choose one, not both), you can deduct the business-use percentage of:

- Oil changes and routine maintenance
- Tire replacement and rotation
- Car washes (a clean car is part of maintaining rider ratings, which directly affects income)
- Windshield wiper fluid, air fresheners used in the vehicle
- Brake pads, battery replacement

Most drivers do better with standard mileage since it is simpler and the rate is generous, but if you drive an older or expensive-to-maintain vehicle, run the numbers both ways.

## Rider amenities

Many Uber drivers provide small extras to improve ratings and earn tips. These are deductible business supplies:

- Bottled water or mints for passengers
- Phone chargers offered to riders
- Aux cords or Bluetooth adapters
- Seat covers or floor mats purchased to protect the vehicle interior

Keep receipts. These add up to a few hundred dollars a year for most drivers but are fully deductible.

## Parking and tolls

Any parking fee or toll paid while driving for Uber, including waiting in a paid lot for ride requests, is deductible separately from your mileage deduction.

## Uber's platform fees are already excluded from your income

Uber's service fee is subtracted before you receive your payout. You do not need to deduct this separately - your 1099 already reflects your net fares. Double check your annual tax summary from Uber to confirm you are reporting the correct gross amount, not accidentally double counting fees.

## Health insurance premiums

If you purchase your own health insurance and are not eligible for a spouse's employer plan, you can deduct 100 percent of premiums on Form 1040, provided your Schedule C shows a net profit for the year.

## Retirement contributions

A SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k) lets you shelter a portion of net profit from tax while building retirement savings. For 2025:

| Account | Contribution limit |
|---|---|
| SEP-IRA | Up to 25% of net self-employment income, max $69,000 |
| Solo 401(k) | Up to $23,500 employee contribution plus 25% employer contribution |

| Deduction | Estimated amount |
|---|---|
| Mileage (20,000 miles) | $14,000 |
| Phone (35% of $90/month) | $378 |

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I deduct my car payment?**
No, not directly. If using standard mileage, the rate already accounts for depreciation. If using actual expenses, you deduct depreciation of the vehicle itself, not the loan payment.

**What if I drive for Uber and Lyft at the same time?**
Combine both platforms into one Schedule C as a single rideshare business unless you treat them as genuinely separate operations.

**Do I need receipts for everything?**
Yes, for actual expenses and equipment purchases. For mileage, a contemporaneous log from an app like Stride satisfies IRS requirements without needing a receipt for every mile.

**What happens if I forget to track mileage for a few months?**
Reconstruct it using Uber's trip history export, which shows pickup and drop-off locations and can help estimate mileage for those gaps, though this is less reliable than real-time tracking going forward.

## Bottom line

Mileage will almost always be your largest deduction as an Uber driver. Track every business mile from the moment you go online, save receipts for rider amenities and phone costs, and consider a SEP-IRA to reduce your tax bill while building retirement savings. These deductions together can reduce a $50,000 income by over $15,000 before tax is even calculated.
